The bikes left outside the stations are on the map, and you can set off from one.

• A network often reports bikes away from its docks. A switch in the display settings draws them, off by default: their marker is smaller than a station's, a tap opens what the bike is and how much charge it holds, and a journey can start from it — you are standing beside the bike, so there is no walk to it, and the ride ends at a station, where a bike goes back. What those bikes are and whether you may take one is the network's rule, which its feed does not carry, so the application says so and claims nothing.
• A station's sheet says how full the batteries standing at it are, fullest first, and how many bikes the network reports out of service. Past four bikes it gives the two ends, and a row unfolds the station bike by bike. It reads the same feed as above, under the same switch.
• Philadelphia, Las Vegas and Bentonville join the catalogue: 340 networks in 39 countries. Networks whose docks are real but whose feed publishes no fixed capacity were being refused, and their live free-dock count now answers instead.
• The application is lighter by half a megabyte, carrying neither the licence texts it repeated eight times, nor letters it never draws, nor pictures no map ever shows.
• Sideways, the address search and the station list give their answers back instead of spending the screen on their header; a station whose name begins with an accent is filed under its own letter; an Arabic title is no longer cut short; a station's two counts are read out loud; and eighteen networks were being listed under an identifier rather than under their name.
